### Slide 3: Introduction to Guidelines (Chapter 1)

### Slide 4: Preface
- Over 100 companies currently contribute to the Center for Chemical Process Safety, an organization founded within AIChE, for the improvement of process safety
- Many different hazard procedures are available to ensure safety throughout the life cycle of the process
- Since the 90’s, process safety has been an integral facet of process design, now combining with operability and economics
  - A design that is inherently safe is more economical and operable; these facets are iterative
- Introduction to Guidelines (Chapter 1)

### Slide 5: Risk Understanding & PSM Development
- 1989 CCPS report Guidelines for Technical Management of Chemical Process Safety report led to evolution of modern day PSM
  - 4 Pillars of Process Safety  20 elements
- Risk Understanding raises three questions:
  - What can go wrong?
  - What is the potential impact (consequence)?
  - How likely is the loss event to occur (frequency)?
- Introduction to Guidelines (Chapter 1)

### Slide 6: Anatomy of Process Incident
- Incident is defined as an unplanned event or sequence of events
- Modes of Operation include Normal, Abnormal, and Emergency
  - The transition from Normal  Abnormal occurs due to an Initiating Event
  - The transition from Abnormal  Emergency occurs due to a Loss Event
- Initiating Event examples:
  - Equipment failure
  - Human error
  - External event
- Loss Event examples:
  - Ignition
  - Release of material
  - Over pressurization
- This full description is compressed into a Scenario
- Introduction to Guidelines (Chapter 1)

### Slide 7: Purpose of Safeguards
- To stop a scenario from proceeding from initiating event to loss event, Preventative Safeguards are employed:
  - These safeguards function to contain and control unit before emergency operating state is reached
- Safeguards can also be mitigative:
  - Mitigative Safeguards reduce the consequence when the emergency operating state exists
- Introduction to Guidelines (Chapter 1)

### Slide 8: Limitations of Hazard Evaluation
- Completeness: how can all hazards be identified?  They can’t
- Reproducibility: results will differ. Documentation benefit number one.
- Inscrutability: data/information is limitless. Concise summary through consistent logs is documentation benefit number two.
- Relevance of Experience: selecting probabilities for new technology is elusive  error compounds
- Subjectivity: teams’ process experience and thus conversative judgement vary
- Introduction to Guidelines (Chapter 1)

### Slide 9: Preparation for Hazard Evaluations (Chapter 2)

### Slide 10: Foundations: Objectives, Scope, and Information
- The objective of the HEP dependent on the process phase:
  - Process Development vs Operation phase require different tasks
- The scope is not only location-wise (section of process) but also sequence-wise (mode of operation)
  - Most domestic safety standards require regulation of hazardous materials with HEPs
- The information required for an HEP is extensive:
  - In short, company should know about technology, equipment within that technology, and the process chemistry
    - Refer to Process Safety Information Presentation
  - This information accrues through process phase
- Preparation for Hazard Evaluations (Chapter 2)

### Slide 11: Software and Team Structure
- High-quality projectors are necessary for display of numerous documents to allow for fast-paced communication between all team members
  - U-shaped tables are often best as they are centered around this projection screen
  - Hard-copy P&IDs are often distributed for individual reference
- Team itself is typically constructed of Leader, Scribe, and Independent contributors (IC)
  - Team Leader is as talented with communication and coordination as they are the technicalities of the process
  - Scribe should not also serve as IC
  - Numerous different IC’s can be included – often correlating to process phase
- Preparation for Hazard Evaluations (Chapter 2)

### Slide 12: Scheduling and Initial Team Meeting
- Scheduling gets more difficult with larger team size
  - It is best to have an entire week blocked off for continuous meetings
  - Meeting attendance must be documented
- Initial team meeting serves to define scope of HEP and involves:
  - Facility Walkdown
  - Previous Incident Review
  - Orientation
  - Review of available Process Safety Documentation (PSI)
- Preparation for Hazard Evaluations (Chapter 2)

### Slide 13: Hazard Identification Methods (Chapter 3)

### Slide 14: Inherent vs Operable Hazards
- Inherent Hazards are dependent on material properties; these hazards are independent of process conditions
- Most common material properties for Hazard Identification:
  - Toxicity
  - Carcinogenicity
  - Reactivity
  - Stability
  - Flammability/Explosivity
- Operable Hazards are dependent on the process conditions in which the materials are exposed to
- Hazard Identification Methods (Chapter 3)

### Slide 15: Interaction Matrices
- Complexity of inherent hazards multiplies when materials are mixed, requiring Interaction Matrices:
  - Reactions of mixed materials (hermicity)
  - Inherent hazards of reaction-formed products (both desired and undesired)
  - Effect of products on materials of construction
- Matrices are process conditions dependent: multiple matrices are often required
  - Duplicate matrices are also required if material of construction selection is not firm
- Hazard Identification Methods (Chapter 3)

### Slide 16: Hazard Identification Results
- Results of the initial identification of hazards should encompass everything that can wrong within chemical process:
  - List of matières and their properties
    - Alterations of hazard properties under process conditions
  - Interaction Matrices
  - Total quantities of each material
  - Reportable materials (EPA)
- It is best to employ qualitative technique in early process phase that asks open-ended questions:
  - Checklist Analysis technique has shown to be great option, requiring extensive walkthrough of situations
  - Combing a qualitative technique with experience of ICs is often optimal
- Hazard Identification Methods (Chapter 3)

### Slide 17: Worst-Case Consequence Estimation
- In the beginning, the extensive results of a blank-slate hazard evaluation procedure are not known
  -  How much should a company invest in HEP?
- To better predict magnitude of HEP resources, management will look top-down by looking at worst-case consequence
  - Dow F&EI and CEI are crude estimates often cited:
    - Dow Fire and Explosion Index considers property/personnel exposure within proximity
    - Dow Chemical Exposure Index considers health exposure to toxic substance (within proximity)
- Hazard Identification Methods (Chapter 3)

### Slide 18: Hazard Reduction and Inherently Safer Review
- Throughout HEP, engineering of process is revised using 4 strategics approaches, ranging from most effective to least:
  - Inherent: materials and process conditions
  - Passive: non-functioning IPLs
  - Active: functioning IPLs
  - Procedural: human intervention
- Within Inherent Approach, plants explore safer design strategies pertaining to materials and the process conditions
  - Minimize: reduce quantity of hazardous materials
  - Substitute: use different, less hazardous materials
  - Moderate: reduce severity of process conditions
  - Simplify: reduce number of process users
- Hazard Identification Methods (Chapter 3)

### Slide 20: Overview of Non-Scenario-Based HEPs
- These light-duty HEPs serve as intermittent evaluation that can:
  - Break the cycle of repetitive HEP
  - Serve as initial scope for new, unknown process
- Description, Purpose, Types of Results, and Resource Requirements are the pillars of these light-duty HEPs

  - Scale of Process = moderate correlation
  - Labor Estimate = weak correlation
  - Number of Procedures = moderate correlation
  - Number/Complexity of P&IDs  = moderate correlation

### Slide 21: Preliminary Hazard Analysis (PreHA) – (4.1)
- Purpose: PreHA is used in early stages of process when process is not well understood
- Description: formulates list of hazards based on materials/process
- Type of Results: Hazard-Cause-Effect are given qualitative Hazard Category ranking
- Required Resources: design criteria, equipment/material specs + 1-2 people
- Non-Scenario-Based Hazard Evaluations (Chapter 4)
- 7/10/2026

### Slide 22: Safety Review (4.2)
- Safety Review is performed during routine operation process phase for older plants
- Purpose: evaluating operating and maintenance practices of plant
- Description: investigates procedures, equipment standing, protection measure upkeep through EDMS and personnel interviews
- Types of Results: recommendations for prompt adherence to standards
- Resource Requirements: extensive site access, personnel interviews, design basis, plant history
- Non-Scenario-Based Hazard Evaluations (Chapter 4)
- 7/10/2026

### Slide 23: Relative Ranking (4.3)
- This is not an HEP, but rather means of prioritizing HEPs in times of limited resources
- Purpose: to rank areas within a plant according to hazard presences
- Description: utilizes crude numerical methods to quantify a substance within a production unit according to risk
- Type of Results: table with ranking of material/units
- Required Resources: fast process, only one person, MSDS, unit quantities, site access
- There are other notable ranking methods:
- Dow F&EI
- Dow CEI
- TPQ
- Mond Index
- Non-Scenario-Based Hazard Evaluations (Chapter 4)
- 7/10/2026

### Slide 24: Checklist Analysis (4.4)
- Can be performed at any stage in the process
- Purpose: to ensure materials, equipment and procedures are adhering to standards
- Description: involves personnel asking themselves yes/no questions to identify deficiencies in process
- Type of Results: yes/no answer with evidence provided to support reasoning
- Required Resources: access to plant, personnel with significant experience on process, operating practices manual, engineering design basis
- 7/10/2026
- Non-Scenario-Based Hazard Evaluations (Chapter 4)

### Slide 27: What-if Analysis (5.1) - Qualitative
- Applicable towards and process phase in life cycle
- Purpose: ask questions about prominent scenarios, listing subsequent safeguards and consequences to identify action items
- Description: Comprise table of brainstormed scenarios
- Type of Results: table of questions with recommendations
- Required Resources: small group, moderate time
- Scenario–Based Hazard Evaluation Procedures (Chapter 5)

### Slide 28: What-if/Checklist Analysis (5.2) - Qualitative
- Can be preformed at any point in life cycle
- Purpose: scenario  existing safeguards  recommendations
- Description: Combines elements of both analysis:
- What-if has outside-the-box brainstorming
- Checklist has review of previously identified scenarios
- Type of Results: table is produced with initiating events, effects, safeguards, and recommendations
- Similar to that What-if analysis table
- Resource Requirements: varying team size, longer timeframe
- Scenario–Based Hazard Evaluation Procedures (Chapter 5)

### Slide 29: HAZOP (5.3) - Qualitative
- This HEP places emphasis on brainstorming unique hazards through creative combination of guidewords + parameters
- Purpose: identify safeguard deficiencies for abnormal deviations
- Description: Methodically steps through process nodes to identify situations that can produce undesired consequences
- Type of Results: recommended action items and/or further studies to address insufficient protection against abnormal deviations (in table)
- Required Resources: Moderate team size, extensive time required
- Scenario–Based Hazard Evaluation Procedures (Chapter 5)

### Slide 30: More on HAZOP
- Preparation is extensive:
  - Leader needs to define process nodes for methodical evaluation pathway
    - Extended for batch processing sequences
- Combinations of Parameters + Guidewords is limitless:
  - Best to used Library-based approach or knowledge-based approach
    - These narrow down options through equipment heuristics and application of past knowledge
- Most HAZOP tables are organized by deviations although some teams elect to use alternatives:
  - Cause-by-cause, exception-only, action item-only, etc.
- Scenario–Based Hazard Evaluation Procedures (Chapter 5)

### Slide 31: FMEA (5.4) – Semi-quantitative
- Purpose: recommend safeguards to equipment-specific failures
- Description: list all possible failure modes of equipment and their effects on the process
- Type of Results: table consisting of identification, description, failure mode, effect, safeguards (existing), and actions
- Required Resources: plant equipment list, as few as 1 people, moderate amount of time
- Scenario–Based Hazard Evaluation Procedures (Chapter 5)

### Slide 32: Fault Tree Analysis (5.5) - Quantitative
- Purpose: recommend action items from illustration of cause-and-effect relationships of process components faults and failures
  - Failure: needs intervention
  - Fault: self-heals
- Description: For every top event (undesirable event), contributing basic events (component fault/failures) are listed in diagrams illustrating Boolean logic
  - Top Events are typically derived from prior qualitative HEP
  - Cause and Effect relationships should not be too obvious (reserve this for FMEA)
- Type of Results: diagrams showing cause-and-effect relationships through minimum required causations (MCS)
- Required Resources: as little as one analyst, extensive time
- Scenario–Based Hazard Evaluation Procedures (Chapter 5)

### Slide 33: FTA Process (1 of 2)
- Tree Diagram is composed of top events to basic events
  - Organized in rows and columns
  - Top/Intermediate and Basic Events are lettered and numbered, respectively
- Matrix is generated:
  - Letters are substituted with numbers for governed events
    - AND Logic: expands # columns
    - OR Logic: expands # rows
    - Substitute letters in Depth-First Traversal order
    - Eliminate
      - duplicate sets,
      - duplicates within sets [3,3]
      - any set containing single-basic event
  - Minimum Cut Set is Extracted
- A
- B
- 1
- 2
- 3
- C
- Scenario–Based Hazard Evaluation Procedures (Chapter 5)

### Slide 34: FTA Process (2 of 2)
- A: AND Logic  substitution on same row
- B: OR Logic  substitution on next column
- C: AND Logic  substitution on same row
- Minimum cut set: 1, 2, 3
- All 3 of these basic events need to occur for Top Event to take place

### Slide 41: Factors Influencing the Selection of HEPs (6.1)
- Motivation for the Study: purpose – dependent on process phase 
- Type of Results Needed: HEPs produce different results
- Type of Information Available: detailed documentation (PSI) is not available during conceptual design phase  analysts have the authority to recommend different HEP
  - Quality of PSI is critical; don’t perform HEP on outdated P&IDs
- Characteristics of the Analysis Problem: complexity, size, dynamic of process impact study scope:
  - FTA, ETA, CCA  Best for multiple, interacting scenarios
  - FMEA, HAZOP. What-if  Best for single, isolated scenarios
- Perceived Risk Associated with the Subject Process: Will the results be accurate?   Never completely
  - Ensure well-rounded team, use cross-collaboration across sites with same process
- Resource Availability and Preference: Many sites stick to the same technique – this is not advised
  - When deciding technique, required resources should only be tiebreaker
  - Time investment heavily dependent on quality of team
- Selection of Hazard Evaluation Techniques (Chapter 6)

### Slide 52: Risked-Based Determination of Adequacy of 	Safeguards (Chapter 7)

### Slide 53: Basis for Safeguard Evaluation
- Experienced-Based Action Decisions
  - Process Incidents & Near Misses have led to accumulated knowledge base over the years  Standard safeguards are incorporated into Checklist during conceptual design
  - Great ISD practice, less applicable for finite quantification of risk
- Risk-Based Action Decisions
  - Quantifies ability of safeguards to reduce risk to acceptable level
  - Risk Components are found:
    - Frequency: estimate from failure rate
      - Failure Rate: estimated from experience (see LOPA presentation)
    - Consequence: estimate from process hazards
  - Most scenario-based HEPs use Risk-Based Action Decisions
- Risked-Based Determination of Adequacy of Safeguards (Chapter 7)

### Slide 54: Scenarios from Scenario-Based Hazard Evaluations (7.1)
- Goal of HEP: identify all possible scenarios (cause-consequence pairing) in a process
- Starting Point within Scenario continuum differs for each HEP  Figure 7.1
- Not all causes of deviations are known  Why HAZOP is very popular
- Risked-Based Determination of Adequacy of Safeguards (Chapter 7)

### Slide 55: Severity of Consequences (7.2)
- Consequence Severity = Impact of Loss Event
  - Loss Event: irreversible process change
  - Impact: negative effect on people & planet
- Scenario Severity should be evaluated before Scenario Frequency  narrows candidate pool
- Severity is estimated using variety of Methods  - see LOPA Ch. 3:
  - Categorical: discretionary matrix
  - Qualitative: estimates from material properties, personnel presence, & release magnitude
  - Quantitative: advanced modeling of loss event
- Safeguards do not alter severity calculations
  - Quantitative HEPs evaluate severity as stand-alone component
  - Qualitative HEPs do not calculate severity  Couple w/ scenario evaluation
- Risked-Based Determination of Adequacy of Safeguards (Chapter 7)

### Slide 56: Frequency of Initiating Causes (7.3)
- Scenario Frequency is difficult to estimate:
  - It is not known how often vapor cloud explosion will take place
- As such, this component of risk is broken down into product of
  - Initiating Cause Frequency
  - Safeguard Failure Probability (Total)
- Often, Initiating Cause (event) Frequency is tabulated on an order-or-magnitude basis 
- Risked-Based Determination of Adequacy of Safeguards (Chapter 7)

### Slide 57: Effectiveness of Safeguards (7.4)
- Safeguard effectiveness is measured through probability of failure on demand (PFD)
- PFD*, coupled w/ IE frequency, is used to determine Consequence frequency
  - *PFD of mitigative safeguard has no impact on consequence frequency (Consequence has already occurred)
  - Depending on HEP, modification factors can be used for TAR, enabling conditions, etc.
- Risked-Based Determination of Adequacy of Safeguards (Chapter 7)

### Slide 58: Risk Estimation Using Risk Matrix or Direct Calculation (7.5)
- Qualitative HEPs estimate (not calculate) risk w/ tools such as Risk Matrices
  - Y Axis: Consequence Frequency from Order of Magnitude Annual Frequencies
  - X Axis: Consequence Severity from categorical designation table
- Quantitative Techniques use techniques (Minimal cut sets, Numerical methods, etc.)
- Risked-Based Determination of Adequacy of Safeguards (Chapter 7)

### Slide 59: Layer of Protection Analysis (LOPA) (7.6)
- This HEP is an Order-of-Magnitude (Semi-quantitative) technique that allows for moderately thorough evaluation of scenario components
- Risk reduction is suggested based on evaluation of existing/proposed safeguards against company’s established risk threshold
- This technique is very popular, but it, like most quantitative techniques, should be preceded by qualitative HEP
  - LOPA does not identify scenarios
- LOPA Process:
  - Identify Severe Consequences
  - Select a scenario for a severe consequence
  - Identify the initiating event and IE frequency of that scenario
  - Identify IPLs and IPL PFDs of that scenario
  - Determine risk of scenario
  - Compare calculated to tolerable risk
- For more information on LOPA, see dedicated Presentation
- Risked-Based Determination of Adequacy of Safeguards (Chapter 7)

- Develop Meaningful Recommendations (Results)

### Slide 62: Development of Recommendations (8.1)
- Hierarchy of recommendations mirrors GFEDPS hierarchy:
  - Inherently Safer Design (ISD) Proposals are most effective
  - Passive, Preventative measures are quite effective
  - Active, Mitigative measures are less effective
  - Responsive Safeguards are minimally effective
- The more a proposed recommendation is perceived to work, the more likely it will be implemented
  - Steer clear of impracticality!
- Analysis Follow-up Considerations(Chapter 8)

### Slide 63: Prioritization of Hazard Evaluation Results (8.2)
- 3 Types of HEP results:
  - Scenario List: Necessary Context for Actions Item Identification
  - Consequence Description: Necessary Context for Action Item Identification
  - Recommended Action Items: Most Critical type
- Typical Priority of Action Items:
  - Actions items that eliminate ongoing violation of regulatory requirement, corporate/site standard, or good engineering practice
  - Action items that eliminate discrepancy in Process Safety Information
- Some Ranking Mechanisms:
  - Structural Importance (single-IE failures are more common multiple-IE)
  - Risk Categorization (criticality categories 1-4 are assigned)
- Analysis Follow-up Considerations(Chapter 8)

### Slide 64: Documentation of Hazard Evaluations (8.3)
- Documentation of results should be contained in standardized HEP-specific sheet (HAZOP Table, Fault Trees, etc.)
- Additional documentation should be included for future improvement of HEP execution. This allows for:
  - Improved process design
  - Duplication of study
  - Support of other PSM activities
- Regardless of documentation extent, plants should maintain documentation post-decommissioning because it is never known when HEP results will be needed

### Slide 65: Development of a Management Response to a Hazard Evaluation (8.4)
- Every proposed action items need management approval for implementation (even critical ones)
- Management tends to defer action items if they:
  - Are infeasible to implement (cost, complexity)
  - Can be addressed w/ other action item
  - Have faulty engineering (QA/QC necessary)
  - The premise of the operating unit will change (future turndown/shutdown)

### Slide 70: Hazard Evaluations of Processes Controlled by Programmable Systems (9.2)
- Programmable Systems (PS)/Programmable Electronic Systems (PES) are the computer-based systems that connect sensors to final control elements
  - PES include single-loop controllers, distributed control systems (DCS), programmable logic controllers (PLC), digital relays, etc.
- Control Systems (PES) have several failure modes. Study of failure modes found frequencies 

    - Software: logic within controller
    - Hardware: components that hoist software (sensor, controller, control element) – controllers have lowest failure rate
- HEP of PES
  - Often, control strategy (program description) is directly evaluated for its impact towards addressing hazard
    - Example: CHAZOP (see HAZOP Presentation: Ch. 10.1)

### Slide 74: Human Factor and Human Reliability Analysis (9.5) (2/2)
- Purpose: identity potential human errors, their underlying causes, and their effects
- Types of Results: proposed modifications to reduce likelihood of most critical errors identified in this HEP
- Resource Requirements: analysts familiar w/ processes who can interview site personal about key operating steps.  Typically takes 1-3 weeks depending on process size.
  - HRA should be performed alongside traditional HEP

- Analysis Procedure:

  - Evaluate Human-Machine Interfaces
  - Performing a Task Analysis of Intended Operator Functions
  - Performing Human Error Analysis of Intended Operator Functions
